The Census Bureau fields 47 demographic survey instruments containing approximately 7,000 questions, each designed for a specific analytical purpose. No comprehensive map has existed showing where these surveys overlap, where their questions are compatible, and where the differences matter. Building that map manually requires a research team to compare thousands of questions across dozens of instruments, a task that takes months and exceeds practical limits not because of complexity, but because of scale. No individual, however expert, holds 7,000 questions in working memory at once.

This research uses AI-assisted methods to build that map at scale: multiple models from competing vendors independently rate question pairs, with structured arbitration for disagreements. Across two major survey pairs (CPS and FoodAPS, each scored against ACS as an anchor survey), 275 unique source questions shared enough topical ground with ACS to be compared, and approximately 52% of those have at least one viable harmonization path. Roughly 19% are directly interchangeable through simple recoding, roughly 33% are linkable with documented statistical adjustments, and roughly 48% measure fundamentally different constructs. Measured against each full instrument, questions with a harmonization path represent about 41% of CPS and 12% of FoodAPS, the gap reflecting how much of each survey's content shares no subtopic territory with ACS at all. Agreement between independent models from competing vendors is measured rather than assumed: post-arbitration agreement on harmonization judgments reached κ = 0.84, and a confirmation run one model generation later agreed with the original analysis on which pairs are harmonization candidates for 88% to 91% of shared pairs (κ = 0.70 to 0.72). The results are not driven by any single model's bias. Among the questions that had ACS territory to be compared against, the rate at which a harmonization path is found is similar for both surveys, 54.8% for CPS and 47.5% for FoodAPS, suggesting the rate reflects shared conceptual territory rather than a survey-specific artifact. The complete analysis was conducted in weeks at a total API cost under $100. The full pipeline, data, and code are public and reproducible.
